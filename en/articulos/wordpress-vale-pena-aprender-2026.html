WordPress in 2026: still worth learning or already "dead"?

This is not a tutorial or a generic “best practices” list: it is what happened to me as a student, freelancer and someone who still uses it when the project calls for it.

WordPress has a curious trait: the more years pass, the more people declare it dead on social… and the more years pass, the more businesses keep making money with it. After more than a decade in tech — infrastructure, web, marketing, automation and now stronger in data and systems — I still see it in production, not in theory.

What many students miss at first is that the feed does not pay the bills. TikTok can motivate you; LinkedIn can intimidate you; what usually pays is a site that ranks, sells and does not fall over on a Friday at six.

How I got in: Joomla first, WordPress after

I found it between 2007 and 2008, still at university and starting to take the web seriously. Before that I had touched Joomla, everywhere back then and it taught me a lot about content structure and templates. When I tried WordPress I felt a different speed: less friction to build something presentable, fewer excuses not to deliver, and an easier story for the client about what they were buying.

When you are a student, that is not a small detail. Learning as a hobby is fine; learning something that helps you pay for transport, internet or a PC part changes the conversation. My first jobs were simple sites for small businesses that only wanted “to be on the internet.” Purists look down on them; the business owner, when new WhatsApp messages show up, usually has another opinion. In Latin America — and in Venezuela especially — the gap between what is “technologically elegant” and what the street needs shows up every day.

The problem of learning only from the timeline

I will be direct: in 2026 there is an obsession with sounding advanced before you know how to hold a project. AI, microservices, Kubernetes, Rust, the framework that dropped yesterday… fine, all of that matters. I am here in Madrid refocusing studies and practice thinking about how to carry that into projects and teaching later.

But some people need a tool that works on Monday first. WordPress is still one of the most honest doors into the web because it forces you to deal with hosting, domains, content and clients without hiding behind too many complexity layers from day one.

The monster some people underestimate

Talking about WordPress no longer sounds “cool” in certain circles, but the volume of sites running it is still ridiculous: media, shops, universities, real estate, restaurants. Most SMBs do not need forty-eight microservices in Docker; they need to appear on Google, project credibility, publish without calling a developer for every comma, connect forms and WhatsApp, launch a store when it is time and not melt in maintenance.

There WordPress is still genuinely useful — not because it is perfect, but because it fits human-sized problems.

“But that is not programming”

I have heard it for years. It sounds like a closed door more than a useful definition. Tech is not for looking smart on a tweet; it is for solving things. With WordPress you enter through hosting, DNS, databases, basic SEO, performance, plugins, some PHP if you leave the editor, UX when you care that people do not leave in three seconds, and marketing when you discover that “publishing” is not the same as “selling.” It is not a speed race; it is a map many never finish because they only memorize tool names.

The mistake of waiting to be senior before you start

I know the pattern: “first four frameworks, flawless backend, cloud and a magazine portfolio.” Meanwhile another classmate shipped three WordPress sites, learned SEO in the trenches, got paid and talked to real clients. Practice with people — deadlines, nerves, emails at ten at night — changes your head more than another course with nothing delivered.

Venezuela and the size of the real problem

This goes separately for anyone studying from there: you do not always need the newest stack for the first paid job. Thousands of businesses still need digitizing: bakeries, clinics, small shops, independent teachers, personal brands. Many do not need a “full stack” app with buzzwords; they need a site that represents them and brings orders.

Whoever combines WordPress with good content, honest SEO, some design (Canva counts), AI as support and basic analytics can offer a very sellable package today. Not because it is trendy: because it solves.

WordCamp Madrid: the conversation is no longer just “pages”

I was at WordCamp Madrid recently and liked seeing the discourse mature: less posturing about “I only install themes,” more automation, performance, accessibility, security, headless when it makes sense, APIs, content flows with AI as help — not as a substitute for judgment.

My read is simple: AI is not killing WordPress; it is changing how we produce and maintain content. Whoever can use both with judgment wins.

AI and WordPress in 2026

AI cuts repetitive work: post ideas, drafts, proofreading, SEO outlines, translations, FAQs, snippets. That helps if you study or bill by project. The problem is elsewhere: there is more and more text that sounds the same. Search engines and people start to reward what smells like lived experience.

The content I think will matter in 2027

I bet on the human mixed with rigor: mistakes you told, decisions you defended, tutorials with local context. Personal blogs and logbooks make sense again when generic mediocrity is one click away. AI can help you write; it did not live your frustration with a conflicting plugin at two in the morning.

SEO without mythology

WordPress is still a cheap lab to understand how search works: query intent, titles that do not lie, speed, Search Console, images that do not weigh a ton, internal links that guide the reader. With search changing because of AI, whoever understands real users — not just “keywords” — has an edge.

Security: the boring part that saves you

WordPress has shadows; security is the most ignored until someone gets in. Often the problem is not core itself but pirated themes and plugins, weak hosting, weak passwords or sites abandoned for years without updates. I have seen sites go down because of “nulled” plugins from shady sites; it is not paranoid theory.

If you will live off this in 2026, minimum: backups you proved you can restore, updates without fear but with judgment, sensible permissions and some firewall or hardening. You do not need to be a pentester; you need not to be the weak link.

WordPress or Wix?

I will probably earn an awkward comment. The honest answer is “it depends.” WordPress.org gives you control and a long path; Wix is valid if you want to close fast without touching a server. If you want to understand the web ecosystem for real — DNS, hosting, performance, migrations — WordPress asks more and teaches more.

WooCommerce in Latin America

WooCommerce is still a serious option for shops that need flexibility without giving up content around the catalog. There are newer platforms; there are also businesses that need to adapt shipping, payment methods and pages that tell a story. Flexibility counts there.

What WordPress gave me

You do not have to start as a reference. Sometimes a “simple” tool is what puts you in touch with clients, brands and real problems. It helped me earn while studying, understand small businesses, learn SEO in combat and go deeper on the web without waiting for a magic degree.

In 2026 I still recommend it as a door — not as a ceiling. Combine it with solid fundamentals: some databases, Linux when you can, content that does not sound like a brochure, analytics you read instead of only installing the pixel, and AI as copilot.

Break staging environments, document what you learn and do not lose your voice trying to sound like the LinkedIn feed. The internet already has enough uniform noise; what is missing is people telling how it really went. If later you want me to add your WordCamp photos or project shots, we plug them into these same slots without fuss.

Context

At the end of 2025 and into 2026, several voices on Twitter/X repeat that WordPress “died.” Meanwhile public reports from W3Techs still show it as the most used CMS on the web, WordCamps fill auditoriums in Madrid, Bogotá and Buenos Aires, and search engines still index like crazy sites running on it. The fight is not “WordPress yes or no”: it is “where do you fit with this in 2026?”

Data

I am not pasting percentages with decimals: on the network they change every month and this text would lie quickly. If you want today’s number, open W3Techs, BuiltWith or HTTP Archive and read how they measure (one is not the same as another).

Without magic numbers: there are still a huge number of WordPress sites, the community still fills events like WordCamp, and in real work it still shows up at SMBs that only want to publish without building a platform team.

As a student

If you are learning, WordPress is an honest playground: it makes you touch hosting, DNS, database, templates and SEO without hiding behind layers. My recommendation:

  1. Set up a local install with Local or Lando — free, in 10 minutes.
  2. Publish something real (your portfolio, a course blog).
  3. Document every problem you break: that is the learning you can charge for.

As a teacher

WordPress is a perfect excuse to teach cross-cutting skills: hosting, domains, accessibility, writing for the web, basic metrics. What works for me in class and tutoring:

  • Assessment by real deliverable: each student publishes a site with a free domain (*.wordpress.com) and defends it in front of the group.
  • Short honest rubric: structure (20%), content (30%), performance measured with PageSpeed (20%), basic accessibility (15%), oral defense (15%).
  • Anecdote I always tell: the first site I built for a family business had a slider plugin that weighed more than the rest of the site combined. They learned in 10 minutes why you measure.
  • Asking for a weekly error log is worth more than a multiple-choice exam.

1) rubric of 5 criteria with weights, 2) feedback in a direct but
respectful tone, 3) three questions for oral defense. If you see weak
performance or accessibility, mark them high/medium/low priority.

As a professional

In my day job, WordPress still appears on projects where the client needs editorial autonomy without depending on me every time they want to change a comma. My flow:

  • WordPress + cache + CDN + some hardening = fast stable sites.
  • For clients with a heavier stack, WordPress as headless feeding a front in Astro or Next.js.
  • For small/medium shops, WooCommerce is still cheaper and more maintainable than migrating to Shopify when the catalog is <500 SKU.
  • What I no longer do: lay out pages with heavy builders (Elementor, etc.) on sites where the client will not edit. Native blocks or a theme with custom CPTs — cleaner, faster.
